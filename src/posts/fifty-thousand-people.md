Every founder is asked the same question, and it is almost always about money. What is the valuation. What is the round. What is the exit.

I have a different number, and I would rather be measured against it.

**Fifty thousand people employed, within ten years.**

Not users. Not downloads. Not a figure on a cap table. Fifty thousand people who go to work somewhere because a thing we built exists, and who go home at the end of the month able to pay for what their family needs.

## Why that number and not another

I grew up watching what a job does to a household. Not a good job in some abstract sense — just a job. Steady, predictable, enough. It changes what a family is able to plan for. It decides whether a child's education is a decision or a dream. It is the difference between a household that can absorb a bad month and one that cannot.

A company that makes a few people very rich and employs eleven of them is an achievement. I am not dismissing it. But it is not the achievement I am trying to have.

The person I keep coming back to is Ratan Tata. Not because of scale, though the scale is extraordinary. Because of a specific habit of mind: he built as though the company existed to serve the country it was in, and the returns were a consequence of doing that properly rather than the point of doing it at all. He was not performing that. He simply ran it that way for decades, including in the years when nobody was watching and it cost him something.

> The companies that last are the ones that behave as though they are accountable to more than their shareholders — long before anybody requires them to.

That is the standard. I am nowhere near it. But a standard you are nowhere near is more useful than a target you have already cleared.

## What fifty thousand actually requires

It is worth being honest that this does not happen through one company. No software business I build is going to employ fifty thousand people directly; that is not how software works, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest arithmetic.

It happens by being useful to sectors that *are* large, that *do* employ at that scale, and that are currently unorganised enough to be operating well below what they are capable of.

That is why the portfolio looks the way it does. Deep technology on one side — sensing, vision, security, healthcare revenue. Ground operations on the other — bullion, infrastructure, agricultural commodities, food processing. People sometimes read that combination as unfocused. It is the opposite. The technology is only worth building if it has somewhere real to land, and the ground operations are where you learn what actually breaks when a system meets a workforce.

Employment at that scale is not created by inventing something clever. It is created by taking a sector where thousands of people already work inefficiently, and making the work worth more.

## The part I do not know yet

I am not going to pretend there is a plan that gets from here to fifty thousand with every step mapped. There isn't. Anyone who shows you that chart is selling something.

What there is: a direction, a set of sectors, a way of entering them, and a refusal to measure progress by the metric everybody else uses.

Ten years is long enough that most of what I believe today about *how* to get there will turn out to be wrong. I expect to be corrected repeatedly. The number is the part that does not move.

Hold me to it.
