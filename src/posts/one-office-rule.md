Most people read a long list of directorships as a vanity exercise. In India it is closer to the opposite: it is a constrained optimisation, and the binding constraint is a single sentence in the Companies Act, 2013.

## One whole-time office, and only one

Section 203 requires certain classes of company to appoint whole-time key managerial personnel. The offices it names are Managing Director, Whole-Time Director, Chief Executive Officer, Chief Financial Officer and Company Secretary. Those five are the statutory KMP offices; nothing else is, whatever a business card says.

The constraint follows immediately. A whole-time key managerial person **shall not hold office in more than one company at the same time**, with one carve-out: they may also hold office in that company's own subsidiary.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is more severe than it first looks. It does not cap how many boards you sit on. It caps how many companies you can be *executive* in. You get one.

## What it rules out

The practical effect is that the common founder fantasy — Managing Director here, CEO there, CFO of the third thing — is not available. Once the MD office is taken at one company, every other relationship has to be something else:

- **Director**, a board seat with no whole-time executive office attached.
- **Promoter**, the statutory term under section 2(69) for a person who caused the company to be formed. It describes origin, not a board seat, and it is the honest word where there is no seat to claim.
- **Co-Founder** or **Founder**, which are business descriptors and not MCA designations at all. They should always travel attached to the statutory term, not instead of it.

The last one is where most public bios quietly go wrong. "Founder & CEO" of four companies is not a bold claim; under section 203 it is an impossible one.

## The second constraint

Section 165 adds a different ceiling: a person may hold at most twenty directorships, of which no more than ten may be public companies. That number is high enough that few people meet it, but it is the reason a sprawling board map is a matter of record rather than a matter of taste. The register is countable, and somebody will count it.

## Offices that are not KMP offices

A point that causes constant confusion: **a CTO is not a section 203 office.** Neither is a COO, a CIO or a Chief Revenue Officer. The five are fixed. So a person who is Managing Director of one company can hold a Chief Technology Officer title elsewhere without touching the one-office rule, because the second title is not a statutory key managerial office in the first place.

Foreign entities sit outside the question entirely. A United States LLC is not a company under the Companies Act, so a role in one is neither a section 203 office nor a section 165 directorship. It should still be disclosed — but it should be disclosed as what it is, marked with its jurisdiction, rather than quietly counted alongside Indian directorships.

## Why state it this precisely

Because the alternative is worse. A biography that lists an impossible combination of offices is not impressive; it is a disclosure that nobody checked. Anyone who deals with Indian company law for a living — a banker, an acquirer, a regulator, a careful journalist — reads the designation before they read the achievement.

> The designations are the cheapest part of the record to get right, and the most expensive part to get wrong.

Stating a board map correctly costs nothing. It means writing *Promoter & Director* where there is no executive office, *Director* where there is a seat and no more, and reserving the one whole-time title for the single company that actually holds it. The result reads as less at first glance, and considerably more on the second.
