## 3. Post one — the ecosystem

This is the opening post. It states the thesis everything else sits inside,
and it argues with something the whole feed believes, which is what makes it
travel.

> Most of the startups being celebrated in India this year will not exist in
> two years.
>
> Not because the founders aren't capable. Many are exceptional.
>
> Because the thing they're building was never designed to last. It was
> designed to be sold.
>
> That isn't a moral failure. It's the natural result of the incentives we've
> built around them.
>
> Somewhere in the last few years, raising money stopped being a step toward
> building something and became the achievement itself. The round is the
> announcement. The valuation is the headline. The deck gets more attention
> than the product.
>
> A founder learns very quickly that the skill being rewarded is narrative,
> not delivery.
>
> Meanwhile — and this is the part that genuinely bothers me —
>
> There are engineers and researchers in this country with a working product
> and a real insight who will never start a company. Not for lack of
> conviction. Because they believe the entry price is lakhs of rupees and
> fifty people on day one.
>
> They've watched the funding theatre and concluded, reasonably, that this is
> what starting looks like. So the thing stays a side project. Or a paper. Or
> nothing.
>
> That belief is wrong, and it's costing India more than any funding winter
> ever did.
>
> Here's what makes me optimistic rather than bitter.
>
> Every part we need already exists.
>
> Innovation, sitting in university labs, substantially complete.
> Angel investors with capital, tired of being pitched narratives, genuinely
> looking for something solid.
> Operators who've scaled real businesses and know exactly where a company
> breaks at twenty people, and at two hundred.
> Advisors with decades of sector knowledge you cannot acquire any faster than
> by living it.
> And an effectively unlimited supply of real, expensive, unsolved problems in
> every sector you can name.
>
> Each of those is strong. None of them is connected to the others.
>
> We don't have a shortage of ingredients. We have a shortage of wiring.
>
> The researcher never meets the operator. The angel never sees the lab. The
> advisor who knows exactly why that sector resists automation is never in the
> room when the product is designed.
>
> One more thing, because I think the confusion starts here.
>
> Everyone repeats: build a great product.
>
> A great product isn't the thing. I've watched beautiful products die and
> ugly ones build durable companies, and the difference was never craft.
>
> Business is solving a problem worth solving — worth someone paying for,
> repeatedly, because the alternative costs them more. The product is just the
> shape your answer takes.
>
> And business is collaboration for collective growth. Not as a sentiment. As
> a structure.
>
> The ecosystem I want to help build is one where a researcher with a working
> result can find an operator, an advisor and a first customer without needing
> fifty people and a crore to begin.
>
> Where capital goes to problems worth solving rather than to decks.
>
> Where the measure of a company is how long it lasts and how many people it
> carries — not what it sold for.
>
> None of that requires inventing anything. It requires connecting things that
> already exist, and doing the slow, unglamorous work of making those
> connections real.
>
> I'd rather spend ten years on that than on a good exit.

## 5. Post three — the pilot

The proof post — the first one in the sequence with named, checkable evidence
behind it.

**Image:** attach `deccan-springs-pilot.jpg` (in dist/assets/, 1200x630). It
carries their mark, the school's name, Hyderabad, and the one line of
substance — deployed, under a signed MoU. A partnership announcement is one of
the few posts where a logo card is the right image rather than a vanity one,
because the logo IS the evidence.

**Before posting:** check Deccan Springs are happy to be named publicly and to
have their mark used. The MoU is a commercial relationship and partners like
being asked. If they would rather not be named yet, the post still works with
the school anonymised — "one school in Hyderabad" — and no image.

> The usual way to enter a sector is to study it, build what the study
> implies, then go find someone to sell it to.
>
> We do it backwards.
>
> Find one partner willing to let you into their real operation. Build for
> their actual constraints. Deploy where failure is embarrassing. Only then do
> you get to call it a product.
>
> In education that partner is Deccan Springs Global School, Hyderabad. Pilot
> deployed. MoU signed.
>
> A school is a deceptively brutal place to build software. The calendar can't
> slip. Users range from administrators to teachers to parents with wildly
> different patience for technology. The data concerns children. And a
> teacher's time is the scarcest resource in the building — any system that
> costs more of it than it returns gets quietly abandoned, whatever the
> contract says.
>
> Which is exactly why it's the right first partner.
>
> A real pilot partner gives you three things you cannot buy:
>
> Truthful failure. A customer says they'll think about it. A partner tells
> you the attendance module broke on the second Tuesday, and why.
>
> Constraint discovery. Every unorganised sector runs on undocumented
> practice. You don't find it in research. You find it when someone says "we
> don't do it that way" and you ask why for the fourth time.
>
> Standing. The second school is a far easier conversation when the first one
> is real, named, and willing to say so.
>
> You can't announce much during this phase. One school isn't a press release.
> For a year the honest answer to "how's it going" is "we're in one building,
> learning what we got wrong."
>
> I'd rather that than discover the same lessons across forty customers at
> once, with all of them watching.
>
> Organising an unorganised sector isn't a campaign. It's one building, done
> properly, repeated.

## 6. Post four — collaboration

The most likely of the four to travel, because it argues with a widely held
assumption.

> People ask who our competitors are. I've stopped giving a satisfying answer,
> because I don't think the question describes the situation we're in.
>
> It's a fair question in a mature market. Demand is roughly fixed, every
> customer one company wins is one another lost. Zero sum. Compete hard.
>
> Almost nothing I work in looks like that.
>
> Take any unorganised sector in India and list what's holding it back. No
> standard way of recording what happened. No reliable measurement. Decisions
> made on memory and relationship rather than data. Skilled people using tools
> that make the skill harder to apply.
>
> Notice what isn't on that list: another company doing it slightly better
> than you.
>
> The binding constraint is that the sector doesn't function yet. In that
> situation a second serious company entering isn't a threat — it's
> corroboration. Evidence to every customer, every bank, every regulator that
> this is a real category and not one vendor's enthusiasm.
>
> Categories get built by several people at once, or they don't get built.
>
> I want to be clear this isn't a soft position. Collaboration is harder than
> competition, not easier. Competing only requires that you move fast.
> Collaborating requires you to be genuinely useful to someone with their own
> priorities, their own constraints, and no obligation to care about yours.
> You have to turn up repeatedly. You have to do what you said.
>
> Most partnerships fail for that reason — not because the logic was wrong.
>
> Our share of a functioning category beats our dominance of one that never
> forms. It's slower. I think it's correct.

*Full piece: bollamrishithej.com/blog/we-do-not-have-competitors*

## 7. Post five — the governance one

The credibility post. Narrower audience, but founders, CAs, CSs and investors
will all recognise that almost nobody gets this right.

> "Founder & CEO" of four companies isn't a bold claim.
>
> Under Indian law it's an impossible one.
>
> Section 203 of the Companies Act, 2013 names five whole-time key managerial
> offices: Managing Director, Whole-Time Director, CEO, CFO and Company
> Secretary. Those five. Whatever a business card says.
>
> And a whole-time KMP cannot hold office in more than one company at a time —
> with one carve-out for that company's own subsidiary.
>
> It doesn't cap how many boards you sit on. It caps how many companies you
> can be executive in.
>
> You get one.
>
> So once the MD office is taken, every other relationship has to be something
> else. Director, where there's a board seat and nothing more. Promoter, the
> statutory term under s.2(69) for someone who caused the company to be
> formed — the honest word where there's no seat to claim. Co-Founder and
> Founder are business descriptors, not MCA designations at all, and should
> travel attached to the statutory term rather than instead of it.
>
> One more that causes constant confusion: a CTO is not a s.203 office.
> Neither is a COO or a CIO. So an MD at one company can hold a CTO title
> elsewhere without touching the rule.
>
> Why be this precise about it? Because the alternative is worse.
>
> A bio listing an impossible combination of offices isn't impressive. It's a
> disclosure that nobody checked. And anyone who deals with Indian company law
> for a living reads the designation before they read the achievement.
>
> It costs nothing to state it correctly. It reads as less at first glance,
> and considerably more on the second.

*Full piece: bollamrishithej.com/blog/one-office-rule*

## Posting notes

- **Order and spacing.** Ecosystem, then fifty thousand, then the pilot, then
  collaboration, then governance — roughly one a week. The first states the
  thesis; each one after it is evidence for a different part of it.
- **The link.** LinkedIn suppresses reach on posts with an outbound link in
  the body. Put the post in full, then drop the article link in the first
  comment.
- **First two lines decide everything.** They're all that shows before "see
  more" on mobile. Each draft above is built so those two lines stand alone.
- **Tagging.** The pilot post is the one to tag Deccan Springs Global School on —
  and worth checking they're happy to be named publicly first, since the MoU
  is a commercial relationship.
- **Replies are the work.** More reach comes from answering comments properly
  in the first hour than from anything in the post itself.
- **Don't add hashtag blocks.** Two at most, if any. They read as reach-seeking
  and this writing is better without them.
